What can I cook with what I have?

Type the ingredients in your kitchen. Two measures are combined with their harmonic mean (the F-measure, as used for precision and recall): coverage = how much of the recipe you already have, and usage = how many of your ingredients the recipe actually uses.

How good is each model?

Every model answers the same labelled test queries. Precision@10 asks how clean the top 10 is, Recall@10 how much of the relevant set was found, MAP rewards putting relevant recipes early.

How a query becomes a ranked list

  
  
    
    
  1. Text operations. Query and recipes go through the same pipeline: tokenise → remove stop words (English + recipe words like cup, tbsp, chopped) → lemmatise (tomatoes → tomato) → normalise ingredient names (dahi → curd).
    2. 
    
  2. Inverted index. Each term maps to the recipes containing it and how often. A lookup replaces a scan of all recipes.
    3. 
    
  3. Vector Space Model. Recipes and the query become TF-IDF vectors. Score = cosine of the angle between them, so long recipes are not favoured for length.
    4. 
    
  4. Boolean model. Filters and operator queries (paneer AND (butter OR cream) NOT onion) are parsed with the shunting-yard algorithm into postfix and evaluated as set intersection, union and complement over posting lists. The result is a candidate set, not a ranking.
    5. 
    
  5. BM25. A probabilistic-family ranking: TF-IDF with term-frequency saturation (k1) and document-length normalisation (b).
    6. 
    
  6. Query expansion. Typed terms weigh 1.0. A thesaurus (📖) adds hand-written related terms at 0.5; automatic global analysis (🔗) adds the terms that co-occur most with each query term across the whole collection, measured by Jaccard c(u,v)/(nu+nv−c(u,v)), at 0.4. This is how "biryani" also retrieves recipes indexed under "basmati" or "salan".
    7. 
    
  7. Rocchio relevance feedback. Mark results 👍/👎 and the query vector moves: q′ = α·q + (β/|Dr|)·Σ relevant − (γ/|Dn|)·Σ non-relevant with α = 1, β = 0.75, γ = 0.15. Terms from liked recipes enter the query (expansion) and existing terms change weight (re-weighting); negative weights are dropped and the 30 strongest terms are kept. Works for VSM and BM25.
    8. 
    
  8. Semantic layer (MiniLM). TF-IDF and BM25 can only match shared words. MiniLM is a small pre-trained neural model that turns any text into a 384-number vector so that similar meanings are close; recipes are encoded once and cached. Ranking is still cosine similarity — only the vectors changed. This is how "cold drink for a hot afternoon" finds lemonades and sharbats.
    9. 
    
  9. Score fusion (result merging). BM25 scores are unbounded, cosines live in [0, 1]. Each list is min-max normalised then combined: fused = w·BM25′ + (1 − w)·MiniLM′ (default w = 0.6). The same technique a distributed IR broker uses to merge lists from different collections.
    10. 
    
  10. Why this result? The per-term table shows tf, idf, both weights and their product; the products sum to the dot product, divided by the two norms to give the cosine.
    11. 
    
  11. Evaluation. Precision@10, Recall@10 and Mean Average Precision on 20 labelled queries let us compare all models with numbers rather than opinions.
